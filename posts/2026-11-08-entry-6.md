*An account of ten days spent upon the island of Oʻahu, where nature proved magnificent, the provisions abundant, and the affairs of men predictably troublesome.*

Our days upon the island unfolded with considerably less regard for my carefully constructed itinerary than I had originally intended. This was not, I hasten to explain, the result of poor planning. Rather, Hawaii possesses an extraordinary abundance of distractions, and I have never considered it wise to remain enslaved to a schedule when fortune presents a more interesting proposition.

Thus our adventures proceeded according to a system of calculated improvisation, a method in which I possess considerable expertise.

We climbed the ancient volcanic heights of Diamond Head, where the morning sun illuminated Waikīkī beneath us. The ascent was agreeable, particularly with the assistance of my mechanical Hypershell, a contrivance acquired during my earlier travels through China. Martina accompanied me, and together we admired the coast while I attended to the important business of photography.

Koko Head proved a considerably more formidable adversary. Its thousand and more railway ties ascended the mountainside with such merciless determination that I began to suspect their architect had harbored a profound resentment toward mankind. Yet with the assistance of my mechanical apparatus and the application of my considerable resolve, I attained the summit. The view was magnificent, though I maintain that the mountain might have furnished an equally satisfactory spectacle with half the required suffering.

Our wanderings carried us across the windward coast, where the Mokulua islets rose from the turquoise waters beyond Lanikai. We climbed the Pillbox ridge in the early light, visited the tranquil Byodo-In Temple, and wandered among the astonishing green mountains of Kualoa. The great cliffs appeared almost impossibly arranged, as though some extravagant deity had sculpted them for the express purpose of impressing travelers.

At Makapuʻu we surveyed the vast Pacific, while at Halona we watched the sea force its way through the volcanic shore. We explored Kailua and its neighboring beaches, where the waters possessed colors which I would ordinarily accuse a painter of inventing.

The northern coast offered wonders of a different character.

At Laniākea Beach we encountered a magnificent Hawaiian green sea turtle, known locally as a *honu*, resting upon the sand with the indifference of an ancient sovereign. The creature appeared entirely unmoved by the admiration of its visitors, an attitude I found thoroughly respectable. Here was a being which commanded attention without making the slightest effort to solicit it. I have known men who spent their entire lives attempting to cultivate such dignity, only to be surpassed by a reptile whose principal ambition appeared to be remaining comfortably upon the beach.

We observed it respectfully from a distance, allowing the creature its peace. I departed with considerable admiration for its philosophy of indifference, though I confess that I have seldom found occasion to practice it myself.

In Waimea Valley we walked beneath dense tropical greenery toward the waterfall, and from the heights of the Ehukai Pillbox we surveyed the celebrated Banzai Pipeline, where enormous waves broke upon the shore with sufficient violence to discourage my participation.

I have never considered it necessary to risk drowning merely to demonstrate that I possess courage. My survival, after all, is of considerably greater value to the world.

We wandered through the banyan trees at Kawela Bay, where I photographed Martina beneath their extraordinary branches. Later, upon the sands of Sunset Beach, I made further portraits as the golden light faded and a nearly full moon rose above the coast.

These were among my favorite moments of the journey. Martina possesses a natural ease before the camera, and I found great pleasure in preserving her likeness amid such remarkable surroundings. The photographs will endure long after the warmth of those evenings has become a distant memory.

Our explorations also led us into the rainforests above Mānoa, where the earth appeared determined to conceal itself beneath an extravagant quantity of vegetation. We walked through bamboo forests toward Mānoa Falls, wandered the grounds of Lyon Arboretum, and followed damp trails through the green valleys above the city.

At Foster Botanical Garden we encountered ancient trees of immense proportions, while Hoʻomaluhia presented a tranquil lake beneath the towering Koʻolau mountains. The landscape was so extravagantly green that I briefly suspected some botanical enchantment had escaped the supervision of its creator.

At Pali Lookout, the wind attempted to remove us bodily from the mountain. Having already endured the hostility of the atmosphere during our arrival, I regarded this renewed aggression with contempt and refused to be intimidated.

Our adventures were not confined to mountains and forests.

We spent considerable time wandering through Chinatown, exploring galleries, curious shops, and bakeries filled with pastries of every description. We visited ʻIolani Palace, where the history of the Hawaiian monarchy offered a sobering account of sovereignty, ambition, and the consequences of power exercised without justice.

The palace was particularly memorable, for it reminded me that beneath the island's agreeable climate and magnificent scenery lies a history far more complicated than the entertainments offered to visiting travelers.

Naturally, our explorations required frequent nourishment.

At Leonard's we obtained malasadas so fresh that the sugar seemed scarcely prepared to accept its responsibilities. At Marukame we enjoyed steaming bowls of udon, while Morning Catch provided poke of such quality that we found ourselves returning for another meal.

We ate shrimp in Kahuku, an undertaking which produced considerable satisfaction and an equally considerable quantity of sauce upon our fingers. The shrimp were magnificent, though the meal required a degree of manual involvement which might have offended a more delicate constitution. Fortunately, I possess no such weakness.

We sampled poke and shave ice along the windward coast, and breakfasted at Kalapawai and Koko Head Cafe, where the provisions proved particularly welcome after our various mountain ascents.

Our evening meals were a subject of considerable interest, though not every celebrated establishment proved deserving of its reputation.

The Vietnamese cooking at The Pig and the Lady was excellent beyond reasonable dispute, while Senia likewise demonstrated considerable culinary talent.

Fête, however, was another matter entirely.

I had approached the establishment with expectations encouraged by its considerable reputation. What followed was a meal of perfectly respectable mediocrity. Nothing was especially offensive, yet nothing inspired admiration. The food was merely adequate, an unfortunate achievement for a kitchen which appeared to regard itself as something rather more distinguished.

I found the experience decidedly middling.

Curiously, its sister establishment, Heyday, situated within the White Sands Hotel, proved vastly more agreeable.

Here I discovered a burger of such satisfying character that I returned upon several evenings to renew our acquaintance. Some might regard repeated consumption of the same dish as evidence of limited imagination. I consider it the conduct of a man who has identified a reliable pleasure and sees no advantage in gambling upon an inferior alternative.

More remarkable still was the Bloody Mary.

I have consumed this particular concoction in numerous establishments, and my standards have become correspondingly severe. Yet the version served at Heyday was of such excellence that I placed it confidently among the ten finest I have encountered.

A considerable distinction, and one which I do not bestow lightly.

Thus I found myself in the peculiar position of preferring the supposedly lesser establishment to its more celebrated relation. An instructive reminder that reputation is merely the opinion of others, while good judgment is the privilege of those fortunate enough to possess it.

Then there was the matter of tonkatsu.

Martina had previously declared one preparation to be the finest of her life, an assessment I respected while declining to endorse with equal enthusiasm. Later, at Tamafuji, we encountered pork cutlets of such exceptional crispness that I was obliged to reconsider the standards by which such dishes ought to be judged.

I shall not claim that we reached a definitive agreement. A relationship between two intelligent persons must allow for occasional differences of opinion, particularly concerning matters as consequential as fried pork.

Yet not every undertaking proceeded according to our designs.

We had arranged an expedition to the Big Island, intending to inspect its volcanic wonders. Lodging had been secured, a carriage reserved, and dinner arranged at the Fish Hopper. Dee accompanied us, bringing the practical wisdom of a man who had previously endured the incompetence of commercial airlines.

Approximately ten minutes before boarding, our flight was canceled on account of a mechanical difficulty.

What followed was an hour of waiting, a telephone conversation with an agent whose speech advanced more slowly than continental drift, and a series of proposals which failed to satisfy even the most charitable interpretation of reasonable travel arrangements.

I demanded a manager, examined the alternatives, temporarily secured a later flight, and ultimately obtained full refunds for our party.

The volcano remained unseen.

I regarded this as a regrettable failure on the part of the airline, though the recovery of our money offered some consolation. A man may be deprived of an adventure, but he need not permit the responsible parties to profit from his disappointment.

With our afternoon unexpectedly liberated, Martina and I purchased inexpensive snorkeling equipment and entered the sea.

Beneath the surface we discovered an astonishing abundance of fish moving among colorful coral. The underwater world proved so lively that I began to suspect we had obtained the better portion of the day's entertainment after all.

We concluded the afternoon with photographs of the sunset, followed by food and drink at our hotel.

Thus the airline's incompetence had inadvertently provided us with an agreeable day beside the sea. I would not recommend its methods, but I could scarcely dispute the outcome.

Throughout the journey, our good friend Dee remained a most welcome companion. His generosity, humor, and familiarity with the island greatly enriched our travels. I have praised this gentleman before and see no reason to diminish my assessment now. It is a rare privilege to find a friend whose company remains agreeable across such a variety of circumstances, from excellent meals to bureaucratic disasters.

Martina also found opportunities for her own discoveries, including a visit to Aaila, where she selected a perfume after careful consideration. I admired her discernment, though I confess that my own attention was frequently divided between the fragrances, the surrounding shops, and the possibility of our next meal.

As our journey approached its conclusion, I devoted further attention to photography. At Magic Island we watched the sunset illuminate the coast, while Kaimana Beach offered a final morning view of Diamond Head.

We also visited Photobox in Ala Moana, where Martina and I obtained portraits together. Having spent much of the journey photographing landscapes and my companion, I found it fitting that we should preserve at least one record in which we both appeared.

At last, the hour of departure arrived.

We returned our elderly but dependable Turo carriage to South Drive and proceeded to the airport, where an airship awaited to carry us back to San Francisco.

I considered the journey as a whole.

We had climbed mountains, wandered through ancient forests, explored unfamiliar neighborhoods, encountered creatures of land and sea, and eaten with an enthusiasm which I consider entirely appropriate to a successful expedition.

We had laughed with a trusted friend, discovered remarkable places, and accumulated photographs which would preserve a portion of our experiences against the inevitable erosion of memory.

Not everything had proceeded as intended. Certain destinations escaped us, arrangements were altered, and one particularly ambitious expedition ended before it began.

Yet I have come to suspect that a journey's worth cannot be measured solely by the number of objectives completed.

The world is too large, its diversions too numerous, and its inhabitants too unpredictable for even the most carefully constructed itinerary to survive entirely intact.

A clever traveler must therefore possess not only the foresight to make plans, but the judgment to abandon them when circumstances offer something better.

In this respect, I consider our Hawaiian expedition an unqualified success.
