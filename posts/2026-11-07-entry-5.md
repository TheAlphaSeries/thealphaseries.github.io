There are few places in this world where the very air seems pleased by one's arrival. Hawaii, I have discovered, is among them.

We descended from the heavens and emerged from the airship into a warmth of such agreeable character that I immediately suspected the island had made certain preparations in anticipation of our coming. The breeze was neither the cruel, bone searching wind of northern cities nor the suffocating breath of some miserable desert. Rather, it moved across the skin with a peculiar gentleness, as though the atmosphere itself had been instructed to receive us with courtesy.

I have traveled sufficiently to know that the winds of the world are, as a rule, ill-mannered creatures. Yet here was a breeze which asked nothing, demanded nothing, and appeared content merely to welcome the traveler. A rare disposition, and one I found worthy of immediate approval.

Awaiting us at the port was Dee, that remarkable gentleman whose virtues I have previously catalogued at some length, and whose continued excellence threatens to exhaust my supply of favorable adjectives.

He had come bearing leis, fragrant garlands of flowers which he placed upon us in accordance with the customs of the island. I accepted this distinction with the grace befitting a man accustomed to ceremonial honors, though I understood that the gesture was one of friendship rather than recognition of my considerable achievements. Martina, whose delight in such occasions is both genuine and infectious, received hers with evident pleasure. The flowers suited her splendidly, though I suspect the island's entire botanical kingdom would have struggled to produce an arrangement capable of improving upon her natural charm.

Thus adorned, we departed in Dee's carriage toward our lodging.

Our first undertaking was a matter of baggage. We had arrived before the appointed hour at which our chambers were to be surrendered into our possession, a curious arrangement by which an establishment may accept one's coin long before it is prepared to accept one's person. Nevertheless, the attendants offered to safeguard our belongings and convey them to our room once it became available.

I regarded this promise with cautious optimism. Hospitality is a noble profession, but I have learned that promises made across a counter are not always fulfilled with the enthusiasm in which they are delivered.

With our burdens temporarily relinquished, Dee conveyed us to the location of our hired carriage, secured through an arrangement known as Turo.

The vehicle awaiting us was a venerable specimen, having evidently survived a considerable number of seasons and perhaps several changes in the prevailing civilization. Its finest years were undoubtedly behind it, though I saw no reason to hold this against the machine. I have known men of great age who remained useful long after their appearance suggested otherwise, and a carriage need not possess youthful splendor to fulfill its appointed function.

It had wheels, an engine, and every appearance of being capable of transporting us about the island without immediate catastrophe. These qualifications satisfied my requirements. I had not come to Hawaii to conduct an exhibition of mechanical elegance.

Our conveyance secured, we returned to the hotel to inquire after our chambers and exchange our traveling garments for attire more appropriate to the island's generous climate.

Here we encountered our first genuine astonishment.

The attendants had not merely kept their word concerning our baggage; they had conveyed it directly to our room, which was situated upon the third floor. I confess that this demonstration of competence exceeded my expectations. The prospect of dragging our possessions upward through three levels of architecture after a day of aerial confinement had been one I regarded with particular distaste.

Instead, our belongings awaited us in perfect order.

I was sufficiently impressed that I briefly considered offering the establishment a formal commendation, though I ultimately concluded that my continued patronage would serve as an adequate expression of gratitude.

Our chambers overlooked the pool, where other guests pursued their various aquatic amusements with an enthusiasm which suggested they had either recently discovered water or possessed an abundance of leisure. The view was agreeable, the room satisfactory, and the arrangement of our belongings entirely to our liking.

We exchanged our garments for lighter attire, and thus restored to a condition suitable for the tropics, set forth upon our first proper exploration of the island.

Our destination was the celebrated shore of Waikiki.

There we found a great expanse of sand bordered by waters of remarkable color, while the evening light settled upon the coast with the extravagance of a painter determined to exhaust his entire collection of pigments. The sea moved with a lazy confidence, sending its waves toward the shore as though performing some ancient ceremony whose meaning had long since been forgotten.

The beach was alive with humanity. Travelers wandered in various states of dress and undress, swimmers challenged the surf, and innumerable persons stood gazing toward the horizon with expressions of profound contemplation. What revelations they expected to obtain from so much water I cannot say, though I myself found the spectacle sufficiently impressive without requiring it to disclose any cosmic secrets.

Martina and I walked along the shore in Dee's company, taking in the sights and the agreeable disorder of the place. I found particular satisfaction in having at last reached this distant corner of the world with two people whose company I value so highly.

There are journeys which become tedious through the inadequacies of one's companions. This, I was pleased to observe, would not be among them.

We walked for a considerable distance, and although the scenery remained pleasing, our bodies eventually presented a united petition concerning the absence of nourishment.

Hunger is a most democratic affliction. It makes no distinction between the philosopher and the fool, the nobleman and the beggar, or a man of extraordinary faculties and his perfectly respectable associates. All must eventually submit to its demands.

Our first intended establishment appeared to be one of those fashionable dining houses whose tables must be secured through arrangements made well in advance, perhaps before one has even conceived the journey. I regarded this custom as a particularly tiresome expression of modern civilization. That a man should be expected to predict his appetite several weeks into the future strikes me as an unreasonable demand upon his powers of foresight.

We therefore passed it by.

Fortune, which occasionally displays an admirable willingness to cooperate with my designs, directed us instead toward an establishment specializing in tonkatsu.

Here we were presented with portions of breaded pork, fried to a pleasing golden finish and accompanied by the customary provisions. The meal was excellent, and I consumed it with the appreciation due to a kitchen which had plainly mastered its chosen craft.

Martina, however, was moved to a considerably higher degree of enthusiasm.

She declared the tonkatsu to be the finest she had ever tasted.

This was a pronouncement of no small consequence, for she possesses a discerning palate and is not ordinarily inclined to bestow such honors without justification. I listened to her assessment with interest, sampled my own portion once more, and considered whether some subtle perfection had escaped my notice.

After due reflection, I arrived at a different conclusion.

The tonkatsu was good.

Indeed, it was very good. Excellent, certainly. I could discover no substantial fault in its preparation, texture, or flavor. Yet I could not, in good conscience, proclaim it the supreme achievement of all pork cookery.

I explained this distinction with the delicacy appropriate to a man who values domestic harmony. Martina remained entirely convinced of her judgment, and I, recognizing that further debate would produce neither enlightenment nor additional portions, permitted the matter to rest.

Thus we enjoyed an excellent meal, though history must record that our respective assessments differed by a degree of enthusiasm.

By this hour, the accumulated burdens of travel had begun to make themselves known. The long passage through the heavens, the acquisition of our carriage, the arrangements at our lodging, and our extended promenade along the shore had together exhausted a respectable portion of our vitality.

Even I, whose reserves of determination are considerable, recognized the wisdom of concluding the day's proceedings before fatigue reduced us to creatures of mere instinct.

We therefore resolved to part company.

Dee, maintaining the same generosity with which he had received us, conveyed us once more to the hotel. We exchanged farewells and established our intention to meet again upon the following day, when the island would have further opportunity to reveal its curiosities.

I was grateful for his companionship and assistance. A man may possess all the cunning in the world, yet there remains considerable advantage in having a trusted friend awaiting him upon foreign shores.

Martina, having endured the day's exertions with admirable good humor, soon surrendered herself to sleep.

I, however, remained awake.

Beyond our chambers came the distant sounds of the hotel and its inhabitants. Laughter rose from the pool below, mingling with indistinct conversations and the occasional splash of water. Somewhere beyond the buildings, the sea continued its endless business with the shore.

I lay listening, my thoughts wandering among the events of the day and the possibilities of those yet to come.

We had arrived safely. Our accommodations were secured. Our carriage, though elderly, stood ready for service. We had walked the famous shores of Waikiki, eaten well, and renewed our friendship with a man of uncommon worth.

Beside me slept the woman I love, while beyond our window an unfamiliar island continued its nocturnal revelries.

For once, there was no urgent scheme requiring my attention, no adversary demanding correction, and no calamity from which I was obliged to extricate myself through some brilliant exercise of ingenuity.

I found the absence of these inconveniences surprisingly agreeable.

Still, as the laughter drifted upward from the pool and sleep continued to elude me, I could not help but contemplate the days ahead. An entire island lay before us, filled with strange landscapes, hidden pleasures, and opportunities whose nature I had yet to determine.

It seemed improbable that such a place could remain entirely undisturbed by my presence.
