With so many matters presently in motion, I have decided that further contemplation of the past would constitute an irresponsible misuse of the present. The old accounts may wait. There are expeditions to organize.

In the days soon to come, I intend to assemble a small but exceptionally capable company of men and sail for Treasure Island. Our purpose is singular and, I believe, culturally significant: to capture the finest photograph yet made of the San Francisco skyline. Should we succeed, the resulting image shall be displayed here at some considerably later date, once sufficient time has passed for the public to receive it properly.

For this undertaking I shall require two or three men of various builds, temperaments, and useful dispositions.

First, a land surveyor: one intimately familiar with the city, its peculiar geography, its winds, elevations, secret prospects, and the many locations from which San Francisco most enjoys being admired.

Second, a pack hauler: of sturdy constitution and cheerful obedience, who shall relieve me of those instruments necessary to my art but whose weight I find personally objectionable.

Finally, a charming fellow: of easy speech and flexible principle, whose purpose shall be to secure our passage through ports, gates, checkpoints, and whatever minor authorities presume to stand between us and destiny.

I shall, naturally, assume the role of mystic: consulting the heavens, interpreting the light, inspecting cloud formations with grave concern, and periodically pointing toward the horizon as though privy to information unavailable to ordinary men.

If all perform their duties admirably, glory is probable.

My second voyage shall be grander still.

By airship I shall depart for a distant island, a place reputed to restore both body and soul and return a weary traveler to civilization with an improved complexion and considerably better opinions about life.

I have visited this island before and can personally attest that the legends are true. Indeed, my spirit remains somewhat irresponsibly ignited from my previous pilgrimage. I therefore return in pursuit of those mysteries that escaped me the first time: warm waters, magnificent meals, long and idle afternoons, and that gentle breeze drifting in from parts unknown, carrying upon it the sweet scent of youth.

For an expedition of this importance, one cannot travel with ordinary company.

I shall therefore be accompanied by a woman of singular distinction, my girlfriend.

Her qualifications are numerous and difficult to summarize without embarrassing lesser persons. She possesses excellent taste, formidable curiosity, an instinctive understanding of beauty, and the rare ability to enter a place and immediately improve its general condition. Where I tend toward maps, equipment, unnecessary schemes, and solemn declarations of purpose, she possesses the considerably rarer faculty of knowing what is actually worth doing.

She is neither porter, assistant, nor decorative passenger. She travels under her own authority.

She too seeks the legends.

Indeed, I suspect the island may surrender certain secrets to her which it has deliberately concealed from me. This possibility is irritating, but I have made peace with it.

Fortunately, we shall not wander blindly.

Awaiting us is a guide most trusted, a man whom I consider a close brother and whom my companion already knows well.

It is difficult to describe this man without appearing to exaggerate, and so I shall make no attempt to restrain myself.

He is possessed of such uncommon ability that, were ambition his principal defect, entire institutions might presently operate according to his whims. He could, I suspect, bend the wills of sensible men toward almost any design and have them thanking him afterward for the privilege.

Yet he has chosen peace.

He lives humbly. He crests one mountain only to discover another behind it. He counts the grains of sand and, rather than declaring himself master of the coast, concludes that he has yet to understand the beach.

Such men are inconveniently rare.

His studies are now drawing to a close, and soon he will seek some grander stage upon which to deploy his talents. What form that stage shall take, I cannot say. I only know that the world will shortly have to contend with him.

Before that hour arrives, however, I intend to meet him upon this island one final time.

Thus the voyage carries purposes beyond leisure. I go to restore the spirit, pursue certain legendary gifts, rediscover an enchanted place beside a woman of extraordinary consequence, and share a few more idle days with a brother before the currents of life carry us toward different shores.

There will also, I am told, be drinks with little umbrellas in them.

The island awaits.
