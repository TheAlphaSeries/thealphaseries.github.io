---
title: The Hypershell Entry 1
date: 2026-11-07
---
Among the innumerable contrivances devised by the ingenuity of mankind, there are few which command my interest so readily as those intended to improve upon the limitations of the human body. I have long regarded our natural construction as a respectable but unfinished achievement. The arms are serviceable, the eyes reasonably competent, and the legs, though useful under ordinary circumstances, possess the regrettable habit of surrendering their enthusiasm precisely when the journey becomes interesting.

It was therefore with considerable satisfaction that, during my travels through China some months ago, I acquired a device known as the Hypershell.

This remarkable apparatus, a mechanical arrangement secured about the hips and thighs, has been designed to assist the wearer in the ancient and frequently tiresome business of walking. By means of mechanisms whose inner workings I shall leave to men of narrower but more specialized genius, the device reduces the effort demanded of the legs, thereby allowing its operator to travel greater distances with less expenditure of his own vitality.

A most sensible invention.

For centuries mankind has labored to produce machines capable of carrying him across continents, oceans, and the heavens, yet until comparatively recently has devoted insufficient attention to the indignity of having to propel himself across the ground once he arrives. The Hypershell represents a welcome correction to this oversight.

I first put the device to use during our extensive wanderings through China, where the distances demanded of the traveler frequently exceeded the reasonable expectations of his anatomy. There it performed admirably, assisting me through long days of exploration and confirming my suspicion that mechanical ingenuity, properly employed, may compensate for certain shortcomings in nature's original design.

Upon returning home, however, the device saw regrettably little service.

The preceding year had found me frequently upon the trails, pursuing mountain summits, forest paths, and other destinations which sensible men might reasonably observe from a comfortable distance. This year, by contrast, has been devoted more extensively to travel. My adventures have carried me across oceans and into foreign lands, leaving fewer opportunities for the familiar pleasure of setting out into the wilderness near home.

I do not regret the exchange. A man cannot reasonably complain of insufficient adventure merely because his adventures have become geographically extravagant.

Nevertheless, I missed the mountains.

Our recent expedition to Hawaii therefore presented an excellent opportunity to renew my acquaintance with the Hypershell and subject it to a series of practical trials.

The first occurred upon Diamond Head.

Now, I must make clear that Diamond Head is not, by any reasonable measure, a particularly formidable ascent. Its paths are well established, its summit readily attainable, and its principal difficulty lies in the determination of its visitors to arrive at the same hour. A man of ordinary constitution may complete the climb without recourse to extraordinary equipment, provided he has not spent the preceding evening indulging too enthusiastically in food and drink.

Yet I saw no reason to refuse the assistance of a machine merely because the task was within my natural abilities.

A clever man does not insist upon suffering when he has already purchased the means of avoiding it.

With the Hypershell secured about my person, I ascended the mountain with remarkable ease. The device performed its duties so effectively that the climb seemed scarcely more demanding than an agreeable morning stroll. I reached the summit with sufficient energy remaining to admire the view, operate my photographic apparatus, and contemplate the curious spectacle of other travelers laboring upward through their own unaided efforts.

I regarded them without contempt. After all, not everyone has yet discovered the advantages of mechanical enlightenment.

The true examination, however, awaited me at Koko Head.

Here the mountain presented a considerably less charitable arrangement.

More than a thousand railway ties ascend the volcanic slope in a long and merciless procession, each one seemingly positioned to extract a further measure of effort from the unfortunate climber. The incline grows increasingly severe, and the summit remains visible throughout much of the ordeal, a circumstance which I consider a particularly cruel feature of its design.

There is something deeply offensive about being permitted to see one's destination while being required to suffer so extensively in reaching it.

I began the ascent with the Hypershell secured firmly in place.

The machine assisted me faithfully, though I shall not insult the reader's intelligence by claiming that the climb became effortless. The mountain remained steep, the steps remained numerous, and my lungs continued to demand their customary tribute.

Yet I possessed a confidence which I might otherwise have found difficult to maintain.

I knew that I would reach the summit.

The Hypershell did not abolish the difficulty of the undertaking, but it rendered the difficulty manageable. There is an important distinction between a machine which removes all exertion and one which enables its operator to continue despite it. The former would be a luxury; the latter is an instrument of considerable practical value.

And so I climbed.

Step followed step, the slope grew more severe, and the mountain continued its stubborn refusal to become any shorter. Yet my mechanical companion persisted in its assistance, and at length I emerged upon the summit, victorious over the railway ties and their architect's apparent hostility toward mankind.

The view was magnificent.

Below me stretched the coastline, the volcanic crater, and the broad Pacific, whose waters seemed entirely indifferent to the exertions required to behold them from such a height.

I stood there with considerable satisfaction, though I was not so foolish as to mistake the machine's assistance for an absence of personal achievement. The apparatus had contributed its mechanical strength, certainly, but it was I who had directed the enterprise, endured the ascent, and selected the destination.

A general does not surrender credit for a victory merely because his soldiers possess excellent weapons.

Several days have since passed, allowing me to consider the experience with greater detachment.

I have concluded that Koko Head, though unquestionably difficult, was not the most punishing ascent of my recent travels.

That distinction belongs to the thousand steps I climbed in Zhangjiajie, during our earlier expedition through China.

The comparison is an interesting one.

Both climbs demanded considerable effort. Both rewarded their visitors with views of exceptional beauty. Both appeared to have been devised by persons who regarded stairs as a suitable substitute for human kindness.

Yet Zhangjiajie exacted a greater price.

I recall reaching the heights of those extraordinary Chinese mountains and finding myself breathless for a period considerably longer than I would have preferred. The landscape was magnificent, with immense pillars of stone rising through the mist like the ruins of some forgotten celestial kingdom. Yet the grandeur of the scene did little to persuade my lungs that their obligations had been fulfilled.

Koko Head was a severe undertaking, but Zhangjiajie had tested me more thoroughly.

I record this distinction not as a criticism of either mountain, but as a matter of historical accuracy. A man who has conquered several formidable ascents must maintain a proper accounting of their respective difficulties, lest posterity be deprived of the information necessary to appreciate his achievements.

As for the Hypershell, my admiration for the device has only increased.

It is not a miraculous instrument. It cannot transform a mountain into level ground, nor can it entirely relieve the body of its natural responsibilities. Yet it provides a measurable advantage, and I have always held that an advantage, however modest, becomes formidable when placed in the hands of a sufficiently resourceful individual.

Indeed, I find myself increasingly interested in the future of such things.

The next object of my curiosity is a new generation of spectacles being developed by Google, those ingenious devices which promise to place information directly before the wearer's eyes.

I await their arrival with particular interest.

Some years ago, the same company attempted a similar undertaking, producing spectacles of considerable ambition but uncertain social acceptance. The invention was remarkable in conception, yet the world appeared insufficiently prepared to receive it. Whether the fault lay with the device, its creators, or the public's limited imagination remains a question worthy of debate.

Now the company proposes to return to the enterprise.

I am curious to discover what lessons have been learned.

Have the engineers corrected the shortcomings of their earlier design? Have the intervening years produced improvements sufficiently substantial to justify renewed enthusiasm? Or shall we witness another expensive demonstration of mankind's ability to invent a marvelous object without first determining whether anyone wishes to wear it?

These questions occupy me with considerable interest.

For we have arrived, by all appearances, in that future which earlier generations described with such extravagant confidence. Machines now converse with their operators, assist their movements, and promise to extend the reach of human perception. Devices once confined to speculative fiction have begun appearing in shops, advertised with the casual assurance ordinarily reserved for household appliances.

I find the development enormously pleasing.

There is, of course, a certain absurdity in the spectacle of a man fastening machinery to his legs in order to climb a mountain which he has voluntarily chosen to ascend. Yet I consider this no more absurd than constructing an airship to cross an ocean, or a camera to preserve an instant which would otherwise disappear forever.

Civilization advances through the refusal of ingenious persons to accept inconvenience as an unavoidable condition of existence.

And I, possessing a healthy appreciation for ingenuity, intend to enjoy the benefits.

Let other men insist upon accomplishing every task through the unaided exertions of their natural faculties. Let them climb their mountains with aching legs, consult their maps with downcast eyes, and regard every mechanical improvement as an affront to some imaginary purity of human endeavor.

I shall take a different course.
