# Arrival Upon the Island

My beloved and I have at last arrived upon the island, having entrusted ourselves to an airship of considerable dimensions, questionable engineering, and accommodations evidently devised by a man harboring some ancient grievance against the human spine.

The toll demanded for our passage was extravagant. For such a sum, one might reasonably expect a measure of comfort, perhaps even the occasional indulgence. Instead, we were assigned seats of such unyielding construction that I began to suspect the proprietor had mistaken the transportation of passengers for the punishment of criminals. Nevertheless, I bore these indignities with characteristic fortitude, reasoning that the discomforts of the journey would only serve to embellish the magnificence of our eventual arrival.

My companion, a woman of uncommon intelligence and excellent judgment, had elected to accompany me upon this expedition. This was a decision I regarded with considerable satisfaction, for although I am perfectly capable of navigating the world's treacheries alone, I have discovered that triumphs are considerably more agreeable when shared with someone whose company one genuinely enjoys.

Our passage through the heavens was, for a time, uneventful.

Then the winds, having apparently learned of my approach, resolved to make themselves disagreeable.

A tremendous gale struck the airship with such violence that the entire vessel shuddered upon its course. Its metallic bones groaned, the overhead compartments rattled, and the passengers surrendered themselves to a most undignified display of alarm. Women shrieked, children wailed, and several gentlemen of imposing appearance abruptly abandoned whatever claims they might previously have entertained concerning their own courage.

I alone, or so it seemed to me, maintained a proper appreciation of the circumstances.

It was evident that the atmosphere had taken some personal objection to our passage. I considered this an act of considerable presumption. Having already surrendered a substantial quantity of coin to the airship's proprietors, I was in no disposition to permit a collection of unruly vapors to interfere with my itinerary.

Indeed, I had made certain calculations concerning the matter. The winds, however powerful, possessed no discernible intelligence. I, conversely, possessed intelligence in abundance, together with an inflexible determination to reach the island. The outcome of such a contest could hardly be regarded as uncertain.

Thus I remained seated, regarding the commotion with the composure of a man who had already settled the question of his survival.

My beloved was beside me, and I found additional satisfaction in knowing that whatever absurdities the heavens might devise, we would face them together. She is no timid creature requiring constant reassurance, and I would insult her considerably were I to suggest otherwise. Indeed, I suspect that had the winds presented themselves in human form, she might have offered them a few observations sufficiently pointed to send them retreating toward the horizon.

In due course, the gale exhausted its objections, and the airship continued onward. Whether this retreat resulted from the pilot's efforts, some favorable atmospheric development, or the winds' belated recognition of their adversary, I shall leave to the judgment of posterity.

We descended upon the island at an hour which might reasonably have been assigned either to an afternoon meal or the conclusion of a respectable day's labor. The peculiar displacement of time had thoroughly disordered my reckoning. I briefly considered consulting a clock, but reflected that an instrument incapable of agreeing with my own internal chronology was hardly deserving of confidence.

Upon our arrival, we were greeted by none other than the remarkable gentleman of whom I have spoken so favorably in an earlier account.

I shall refrain from repeating the full inventory of his virtues, lest the reader accuse me of excessive generosity. Such accusations would be particularly offensive, as I am not ordinarily inclined to distribute praise without first establishing that the recipient has earned it.

This gentleman, however, remains a rare exception.

His faculties are formidable, his character unusually sound, and his capacity to influence the designs of other men is such that, were he possessed of a more ambitious temperament, I suspect several governments would presently find themselves operating under his direction without quite understanding how the arrangement had come about.

Yet he prefers a modest existence, displaying that curious restraint which so often accompanies men of genuine ability. I admire this quality enormously, though I confess that, were I endowed with such influence, I might be tempted to employ it toward rather more spectacular ends.

He received us warmly, and presently we departed in his carriage.

Now, it should be understood that I had already secured a carriage of my own for the duration of our stay. I am not a man who arrives upon foreign shores without having made certain provisions for his convenience. Nevertheless, it occurred to us that dispatching two carriages toward the same destination would constitute an extravagance of the most pointless variety.

Furthermore, as guests, we considered it entirely appropriate to accept our friend's hospitality. My own conveyance could remain at our lodging until some future occasion demanded its services.

Thus, through the application of sound judgment and a welcome economy of effort, the matter was resolved to everyone's satisfaction.

There is little else to recount concerning this first day.

No tyrant was overthrown, no fabulous treasure discovered, and no elaborate deception required the exercise of my more exceptional talents. The island, perhaps wisely, offered no immediate resistance to our presence.

Instead, we enjoyed the company of a trusted friend, exchanged stories, and surrendered ourselves to laughter of the most agreeable kind.

I found particular pleasure in seeing my beloved welcomed so warmly. She and our host were already acquainted, and their easy familiarity spared us the tedious ceremony of introductions. There is a peculiar happiness in seeing those whom one holds in high regard enjoy one another's company, particularly when one may take some modest credit for having assembled so distinguished a gathering.

And so our first day concluded without calamity.

We had crossed a great expanse of sky, endured the indignities of commercial aviation, defied the hostility of the winds, and arrived safely upon a distant island. We had been received by a friend whose company I value immensely, and I had beside me the woman whose presence makes even the most uncertain journey worth undertaking.

A lesser man might dismiss such a day as uneventful. I, possessing a more refined understanding of fortune, recognize that not every victory requires the humiliation of an enemy or the acquisition of wealth.

Sometimes it is sufficient to arrive precisely where one intended, in the company of precisely those with whom one wishes to be.
